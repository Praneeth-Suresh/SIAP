Goals, Environments, and Actions

Agency begins with a compact covenant: choose actions that push a world toward a goal. The covenant sounds poetic until we formalize it, at which point it becomes testable engineering [2].

Formal model

Let an agent-environment system be [ \mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{A}, T, \Omega, R, C, \gamma), ] where (T(s' \mid s,a)) is transition dynamics, (\Omega(o \mid s)) is observation dynamics, (R) is task reward, (C) is a constraint vector (cost, risk, budget), and (\gamma \in (0,1)) is discount.

A policy (\pi(a\mid h_t)), with history (h_t=(o_{0:t},a_{0:t-1})), seeks [ \max_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}{\pi,T,\Omega}!\left[\sum\gamma^t R(s_t,a_t)\right] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbb{E}}^{\infty{\pi}!\left[\sum\gamma^t C_j(s_t,a_t)\right] \le \kappa_j. ] Natural-language goals are therefore specifications that must be compiled into (R), (C), and termination conditions.}^{\infty

Three-theory braid

  1. Expected-utility foundations. Rational preference under uncertainty is represented by utility maximization; this turns “what should I do?” into a mathematical objective [33] [34].
  2. Sequential decision theory. MDP/POMDP formalisms model how present action sculpts future opportunity, not just immediate text quality [4] [7].
  3. Feedback control. In open worlds, behavior is closed-loop: observations update state estimates, which update action, which perturbs the world again [36] [39].

How to study this formally

Define benchmark tasks with explicit ((R,C)), estimate transition uncertainty, and compare policies by constrained return, regret, and violation rate. If a system cannot state its objective and constraints mathematically, it has not yet earned the title “agent”; it is still a very articulate reflex.
