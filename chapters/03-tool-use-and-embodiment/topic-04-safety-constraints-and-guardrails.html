Safety Constraints and Guardrails

Between what an agent wants to do and what the world actually suffers stands a layer of machinery. That machinery is what we build here. We will price harm with numbers, fence the state with an inequality, and decide who is allowed to sign off on what. The reward for reading carefully is a safety story you can test, not merely recite.

Safety is not a postscript to capability. It is the part of the interpreter that decides which proposed transitions are outside the program's charter, which are allowed but costly, and which require another authority before the world may be changed. A transition here is one step of the agent loop: the move from the current state to the next state. In a tool-using system, safety lives at the same boundary as action. That boundary sits between the model's proposed next step and the external effect that would make that step real.

This makes guardrails more than warning text. A guardrail is a filter on the agent's transition system, meaning the set of steps the agent is able to take at all. Such a filter has four moves available:

  • remove an action from the action space, so the agent cannot even propose it;
  • reshape the action into a safer one;
  • pause for human approval;
  • attach an obligation to monitor what happens next.

Constrained Control Formulation

Rewarding the Agent While Capping Its Harm

Start with the ordinary picture of a decision-making agent and then add ledgers for harm. Model policy selection as a constrained MDP (Markov decision process, the standard model of an agent choosing actions in states over time): [ \max_{\pi}\ J_R(\pi)=\mathbb{E}\pi!\left[\sum_t\gamma^t r_t\right] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad J}(\pi)=\mathbb{E\pi!\left[\sum_t\gamma^t c\right]\le d_j. ] Read this first as bookkeeping. (J_R(\pi)) reads "the expected discounted reward earned by policy (\pi)", where a policy is the agent's rule for choosing actions, and discounted means later rewards count a little less. Each (J_{C_j}(\pi)) reads "the expected discounted amount of harm number (j)". Those harms are concrete: privacy exposure, money spent, dangerous commands, or manual interruptions. The bound (d_j) is the allowed budget for that cost, and "s.t." reads "subject to".

The contrast is the whole point. A plain reinforcement-learning agent asks, "How much reward can I get?" A constrained agent asks, "How much reward can I get while keeping each harm ledger under its limit?"

That constrained objective is dense with notation, so the following table gives each symbol a short reading before we use it again.

Symbol How to read it
(\pi) The policy: the agent's rule for choosing actions
(J_R(\pi)) Expected discounted reward under that policy
(J_{C_j}(\pi)) Expected discounted amount of harm (j)
(d_j) The budget allowed for harm (j)
(\lambda_j) The price charged for one unit of harm (j)
(h(x)) Safety margin at state (x); nonnegative means safe

Turning Constraints into Prices

The Lagrangian form, which folds a constraint into the objective by charging a price for breaking it, [ \mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)=J_R(\pi)-\sum_j\lambda_j\big(J_{C_j}(\pi)-d_j\big) ] supports dual optimization and transparent trade-off tuning.

A Lagrange multiplier (\lambda_j) is a shadow price for violating constraint (j). If privacy risk is already close to its bound, a large (\lambda_j) makes risky actions look expensive. If the risk ledger is comfortably low, the price can be smaller. Dual optimization is the process of adjusting these prices while improving the policy; a builder implements it as a slow feedback loop that raises a price whenever the measured ledger creeps over budget.

With two actions the idea is visible. Suppose fast_patch yields reward 10 and harm 4, while safe_patch yields reward 7 and harm 1 under a harm budget of 2. When (\lambda=0), fast wins, since 10 beats 7 and harm is free. When (\lambda=2), their priced values become (10-2(4-2)=6) and (7-2(1-2)=9), so the safer action wins because scarce safety budget has become dear. Notice that safe_patch is rewarded for coming in under budget.

Prohibition Is Not the Same as Price

At the state-action level, constrained Bellman structure appears as [ Q^\pi_\lambda(s,a)=r(s,a)-\sum_j\lambda_j c_j(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}{s'}[V^\pi\lambda(s')], ] which reads "the priced value of taking action (a) in state (s) is its reward minus its priced harms, plus the discounted value of wherever it lands you". This makes "unsafe but high reward" mathematically explicit instead of politically vague.

Some constraints are hard: do not leak secrets, do not run a destructive command without authorization, do not fabricate a citation. Others are budgets: latency, cost, bounded uncertainty, allowed retry counts. The mathematics matters because it separates prohibition from price. Hard constraints belong in the action space as removals; budgets belong in the objective as costs.

Runtime Safety Filter

A Fence Written as an Inequality

Define safe set (\mathcal{C}={x:h(x)\ge0}). Control barrier functions require [ \dot h(x,u)+\alpha(h(x))\ge0 ] for class-(\mathcal{K}) function (\alpha), giving forward invariance of (\mathcal{C}) [52]. A practical filter solves [ u^\star=\arg\min_u |u-u_{\text{nom}}|^2 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \dot h(x,u)+\alpha(h(x))\ge0, ] so nominal autonomy is preserved when safe and corrected when not. Here (u_{\text{nom}}) is the control the agent nominally wanted, and (u^\star) is the nearest control that still satisfies the safety condition.

The notation hides a simple fence. The function (h(x)) measures signed distance from danger: nonnegative means safe, negative means outside the safe set. The dot in (\dot h) means derivative, the instantaneous rate at which that safety margin is changing under control (u). A class-(\mathcal{K}) function is an increasing function with (\alpha(0)=0); it lets the allowed rate depend on how much margin remains. Forward invariance means that once the state starts in the safe set, the filtered controller keeps future states there under the model assumptions.

A One-Dimensional Budget Example

In one dimension, let (x) be dollars already spent and let the budget cap be 100, so (h(x)=100-x). At (x=95), the margin is (h=5). A proposed action that spends 10 would push (h) negative, to (-5). The filter searches for the closest acceptable action, perhaps spend 4, ask approval, or wait for more budget. The minimization is the formal way of saying "change the command as little as possible while preserving the safety condition."

The Same Pattern for Language Agents

For language agents, the same pattern appears in less continuous clothing. The nominal action may be "delete the database," "email this file," or "publish the deployment." The safety filter checks state, role, data class, reversibility, blast radius, and evidence. If the proposed action would cross a boundary, the filter blocks it, rewrites it into a reversible alternative, or asks for human approval.

This analogy must be used honestly. A continuous control-barrier proof does not automatically prove that a discrete language-agent filter is safe. To get an equally formal guarantee, the language-agent system would need its own transition invariant: a statement showing that every permitted discrete step preserves the safety property. Without that proof, the barrier picture is a design pattern and a source of tests, not a borrowed certificate.

The safety filter has four branches: propose, classify the risk, then reject, escalate, or execute under monitoring.

proposal = policy(state)
risk = classify(proposal, state)
if forbidden(risk):
  reject(proposal)
elif requires_approval(risk):
  ask_human(proposal, evidence)
else:
  execute_with_monitor(proposal)

The important feature is placement. The filter stands between proposal and effect, not between effect and apology.

Layers of Constraint

Safety architectures are layered because no single check sees the whole risk. Each layer watches a different moment in the loop:

  • Static constraints remove forbidden tools or arguments before planning begins.
  • Pre-execution checks validate schemas, permissions, data sensitivity, and risk class.
  • Runtime monitors watch for drift, unsafe intermediate states, budget exhaustion, and unexpected tool outputs.
  • Post-action checks verify whether the intended safety property actually held.
  • Audit records make repair possible when the system or organization learns that a boundary was incomplete.

Three bodies of theory supply these layers. Constrained MDP theory provides the expected-utility compass with explicit harm budgets. Control barrier functions provide the model of an online filter that preserves invariants. Governance frameworks translate institutional risk appetite into operational envelopes that outlive any single prompt or model version [51] [52] [29] [53]. Together they make safety a property of the agent loop, not a promise in the system message. Topic 3 (Embodiment: Sensors, Actuators, and Feedback) supplies the sensing and feedback machinery that these monitors depend on.

Designing for Escalation

Handing a Decision to a Human on Purpose

Human escalation is not a defeat. It is a designed transition for states where the autonomous policy should not pretend to be sovereign. The interface should answer four questions:

  • what evidence the human receives;
  • what decision is requested;
  • how long the approval remains valid;
  • whether the agent may continue with a safer fallback while waiting.

Allocating Authority Action by Action

Authority is therefore an allocation problem. A reversible formatting edit may be delegated to the agent. A citation replacement may require evidence. A secret-bearing export may require a human. A forbidden disclosure may not be priced at all, because a hard constraint is not an expensive option.

Different tasks deserve different authority envelopes according to reversibility, blast radius, evidence quality, time pressure, and the value of partial progress while approval is pending. Topic 1 (Tool Interfaces and Function Calling) describes the contracts these envelopes attach to. The design error is to flatten all of this into either "the agent is autonomous" or "the human decides." An institution allocates authority at the transition where it matters.

Measuring Whether the Guardrails Work

Evaluate these systems by reporting task return against violation probability, dual-variable stability where constraints are optimized, blocked-action loops, escalation latency, successful fallback rate, and incident quality under correlated failures rather than isolated happy-path tests [33] [34]. Read that list as a dashboard: reward on one axis, violations on the other, with the awkward operational numbers beside them.

A better agent is not the one that says it will be safe. It is the one whose forbidden actions are blocked, whose risky tradeoffs are explicitly priced, whose ambiguous actions escalate, whose waiting states still make useful progress when allowed, and whose incidents generate evidence for repair.
